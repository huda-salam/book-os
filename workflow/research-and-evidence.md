# Research & Evidence Workflow

## Purpose

This workflow converts research into traceable, usable knowledge without collapsing source statements, inference, interpretation, and recommendation into one layer.

The goal is not to collect the largest number of sources. The goal is to establish enough trustworthy evidence to support the book's claims, decisions, explanations, and boundaries.

## 1. Start with a question

Every substantive research activity should begin with a defined question.

Prefer:

**Question → Claim/Decision → Evidence Requirement → Sources → Assessment → Interpretation → Implication**

Avoid starting with a vague instruction such as "find everything about X".

A research question should identify, where relevant:

- what is being investigated,
- why it matters,
- relevant population or context,
- time period,
- jurisdiction,
- required precision,
- intended use in the book.

## 2. Define the claim before searching

Where practical, formulate the intended claim or decision before gathering evidence.

Examples:

- What mechanism explains X?
- Under what conditions does X occur?
- What evidence supports or contradicts X?
- What are the known limitations?
- Which alternatives exist?
- What would change the conclusion?

This reduces confirmation bias and prevents source collection from becoming an end in itself.

## 3. Match source class to claim

Different claims require different evidence.

Consider:

- primary research or original data,
- official documentation,
- laws, regulations, and standards,
- peer-reviewed research,
- empirical industry evidence,
- technical documentation,
- expert analysis,
- reputable secondary reporting,
- practitioner experience,
- anecdote.

Source prestige alone is insufficient. Assess whether the source is appropriate for the specific claim.

## 4. Capture provenance

For material evidence, record at minimum:

- source identity,
- author or issuing organization,
- publication date,
- version or revision date where relevant,
- URL or stable locator,
- jurisdiction or population where relevant,
- scope,
- claim supported,
- evidence strength,
- limitations,
- access/retrieval date when useful.

A citation without provenance is weak research infrastructure.

## 5. Evidence record

A reusable evidence record should follow:

**Claim → Claim Type → Source → Version/Date → Evidence Strength → Evidence Boundary → Interpretation → Implication**

The source statement and the researcher's interpretation must remain distinguishable.

## 6. Claim types

Useful classifications include:

- **Fact** — directly supported observation or documented statement.
- **Empirical finding** — result from systematic observation, measurement, or study.
- **Definition** — meaning established by a recognized source or the book's canonical terminology.
- **Causal claim** — asserts that one factor produces or influences another.
- **Forecast** — statement about a future condition.
- **Interpretation** — reasoned explanation of evidence.
- **Inference** — conclusion derived from available evidence.
- **Recommendation** — proposed action based on requirements, evidence, assumptions, and trade-offs.
- **Unknown** — currently unresolved or insufficiently evidenced.

The classification should match what the evidence can actually support.

## 7. Evidence strength

Do not use one universal numerical score unless the method is explicitly justified.

A practical qualitative scale is:

- **Strong** — direct, relevant, sufficiently independent evidence with clear boundaries.
- **Moderate** — credible evidence with meaningful limitations or indirectness.
- **Limited** — some relevant support, but important uncertainty remains.
- **Insufficient** — evidence does not justify the intended claim.

Evidence strength is about support for a particular claim, not about whether the source is "good" in the abstract.

## 8. Evidence boundaries

Every important claim should be checked for boundaries:

- Who or what does the evidence describe?
- When?
- Where?
- Under what conditions?
- What was actually measured?
- What was not measured?
- Is the result descriptive, correlational, causal, predictive, or prescriptive?
- Can the evidence reasonably be generalized?

A citation does not automatically establish causality, generalizability, or applicability.

## 9. Counterevidence

Research should actively look for evidence that could weaken the intended conclusion.

For important claims ask:

- What evidence would contradict this?
- Are there credible alternative explanations?
- Are there studies or cases with different results?
- Does the source acknowledge limitations?
- Is there a relevant boundary condition?

Counterevidence is part of evidence quality, not an optional appendix.

## 10. Research synthesis

Do not turn a pile of sources into a list of citations.

Synthesis should identify:

1. what is well established,
2. what is supported but conditional,
3. what is disputed,
4. what is uncertain,
5. what evidence is missing,
6. what follows from the evidence,
7. what does not follow.

The final prose should preserve these distinctions.

## 11. From research to book knowledge

Research becomes book knowledge only after it is connected to the knowledge architecture.

Typical transformation:

**Source → Evidence Record → Claim → Concept/Mechanism → Chapter → Reader Question → Implication**

A source should not automatically become a chapter, and a frequently cited idea should not automatically become canonical.

## 12. Canonical knowledge promotion

Promote a research finding into canonical book knowledge only when:

- its meaning is clear,
- its evidence boundary is known,
- terminology is stable,
- its relationship to other concepts is understood,
- its location in the knowledge architecture is clear.

If these conditions are not met, keep it as research material or an explicitly provisional concept.

## 13. Conflicting evidence

When credible sources disagree:

1. preserve the disagreement,
2. identify why they may differ,
3. compare populations, definitions, methods, dates, and contexts,
4. assess relative evidence strength,
5. determine whether the disagreement is substantive or terminological,
6. state what can and cannot be concluded.

Do not manufacture consensus merely to make the chapter cleaner.

## 14. Current and versioned knowledge

For knowledge that changes over time, record:

- effective date,
- publication/revision date,
- version,
- jurisdiction,
- status,
- superseded source where relevant.

This is especially important for:

- regulations,
- standards,
- software,
- APIs,
- products,
- market data,
- organizational policies.

A current claim should not be supported only by a historical source when the current state may differ.

## 15. AI-assisted research

AI may help with:

- query formulation,
- source discovery,
- extraction,
- comparison,
- summarization,
- identifying apparent contradictions,
- generating research questions.

AI output is not automatically evidence.

The workflow remains:

**AI discovery → source verification → evidence capture → human/AI assessment → synthesis**

Do not cite an AI-generated summary as though it were the underlying source.

## 16. Research stopping rule

Research should stop when additional searching produces diminishing value relative to the book's needs.

A claim is research-ready when:

- the required evidence class has been searched,
- major counterevidence has been considered,
- boundaries are understood,
- remaining uncertainty is explicit,
- further research is unlikely to materially change the current treatment.

This is not a claim that the topic is exhaustively researched.

## 17. Research quality gate

Before evidence is promoted into substantive writing, ask:

- Is the claim explicit?
- Is the source appropriate?
- Is provenance captured?
- Is evidence strength assessed?
- Are boundaries known?
- Was counterevidence considered?
- Are interpretation and source statements separated?
- Is the claim placed in the correct canonical concept?
- Does the evidence justify the wording strength?
- Is uncertainty visible?

If not, keep the material in research status.

## Governing principle

> Research is successful when the reader can understand not only what the book claims, but why the claim is justified, where it stops being justified, and what would change it.
