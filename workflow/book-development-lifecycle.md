# Book Development Lifecycle

## Purpose

The Book Development Lifecycle defines the minimum sequence for turning a new book idea into a coherent, evidence-aware, maintainable body of knowledge.

It prevents two common failures:

- starting chapter writing before the intellectual architecture is stable;
- treating drafting, research, review, and release as one undifferentiated activity.

The lifecycle is domain-independent. A new book supplies its own subject matter, concepts, terminology, evidence, examples, and conclusions.

## Lifecycle

```
Intake → Foundation → Architecture → Research → Draft → Integrate → Review → Validate → Release → Maintain
```

The stages are sequential enough to protect quality, but iterative enough to allow controlled return to an earlier stage.

## 1. Intake

Understand what the new book is before deciding how to build it.

Required outputs:

- Book Identity
- Central Problem
- Thesis candidate
- Audience
- Reader Outcomes
- Core Questions
- Purpose
- Scope
- Out of Scope
- Language and style intent
- Depth and horizon
- Evidence expectations
- Intended use

**Gate:** Do not begin substantive chapter writing if the central problem, audience, purpose, or scope is materially unclear.

## 2. Foundation

Turn the intake into an explicit intellectual contract.

Required outputs:

- Book Thesis
- Audience
- Reader Outcomes
- Core Questions
- Knowledge Domains
- Conceptual Model
- Evidence Strategy
- Quality Strategy
- Retrieval Strategy

**Gate:** A reasonable reader should be able to explain the book's purpose and boundaries without reading the chapters.

## 3. Architecture

Design the knowledge structure before filling it with prose.

Required outputs:

- Book Architecture
- Chapter Map
- Section responsibilities
- Canonical concept ownership
- Cross-chapter dependencies
- Evidence dependencies
- Retrieval paths

Each chapter should have a distinct intellectual job.

**Gate:** No major chapter should exist only because it is conventional, and central concepts should not have competing canonical homes without an explicit reason.

## 4. Research

Acquire and assess evidence required by the book's claims and decisions.

Use:

**Question → Claim/Decision → Evidence Requirement → Sources → Assessment → Interpretation → Implication**

Research is claim-driven, not collection-driven.

**Gate:** Important claims must have evidence appropriate to their claim type, with boundaries and uncertainty recorded.

## 5. Draft

Write from the knowledge architecture rather than discovering the architecture accidentally inside prose.

A useful progression is:

**Purpose → Concept → Mechanism → Requirements/Constraints → Alternatives → Trade-offs → Risks/Failure → Evidence → Implication → Practical Application**

Not every section requires every element. Use only what improves understanding.

**Gate:** Draft content must preserve epistemic distinctions and canonical terminology.

## 6. Integrate

Connect newly drafted material to the rest of the book.

Check:

- terminology consistency;
- canonical concept ownership;
- assumptions;
- claims and evidence;
- causal relationships;
- cross-references;
- dependencies;
- contradictions;
- reader progression;
- retrieval paths.

**Gate:** A new section must not silently change the meaning of an established concept, claim, or recommendation.

## 7. Review

Review in layers rather than as one generic proofreading pass.

Required dimensions:

1. Structural
2. Conceptual
3. Evidence
4. Terminology
5. Cross-chapter
6. Red-team
7. Retrieval
8. Editorial

Fix in this order:

**Critical defect → Conceptual → Structural → Evidence → Consistency → Editorial → Stylistic**

## 8. Validate

Validation asks whether the book actually works for its intended use.

Test at least:

- Can readers answer the core questions?
- Can important concepts be retrieved quickly?
- Can claims be distinguished from interpretation and recommendation?
- Can evidence boundaries be found?
- Can a reader understand what would change a conclusion?
- Can a future AI assistant retrieve the book's stated position without inventing one?

Use representative questions, case walkthroughs, red-team prompts, and retrieval tests where appropriate.

## 9. Release

A release is a controlled knowledge state, not merely a published file.

Record:

- version;
- scope of release;
- material changes;
- known limitations;
- unresolved questions;
- review status;
- evidence status;
- language/style profile;
- release date.

Do not release a materially changed knowledge base without understanding its review impact.

## 10. Maintain

After release, treat the book as maintainable knowledge.

Monitor:

- outdated evidence;
- changed standards or regulations where relevant;
- terminology drift;
- broken cross-references;
- superseded recommendations;
- unresolved contradictions;
- reader retrieval failures.

Prefer small, traceable changes over uncontrolled rewriting.

## Iteration Rules

Return to an earlier stage when:

- research invalidates an assumption;
- evidence weakens a central claim;
- a new concept exposes an architectural gap;
- terminology reveals a conceptual conflict;
- reader testing shows the progression does not work;
- a release changes the intended scope.

When returning, record the reason and affected decisions when the change is material.

## Minimum Release Gates

A book is not ready for release when any of these remain unresolved:

- a critical conceptual contradiction;
- a material unsupported claim presented as established fact;
- unclear canonical ownership for a central concept;
- a recommendation presented without relevant assumptions or constraints;
- evidence boundaries that materially change interpretation but are hidden;
- broken retrieval paths for core reader questions.

## Completion Principle

The lifecycle is complete when the book is not merely written, but when its knowledge can be understood, challenged, retrieved, applied within its boundaries, and maintained without losing epistemic meaning.
